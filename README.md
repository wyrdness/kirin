## Overview

The kirin (Japanese 麒麟; Chinese qilin; Korean girin; Vietnamese kỳ lân) is one of the most revered creatures of East Asian mythology and one of the Four Auspicious Beasts (四靈, sì líng) alongside the dragon, phoenix, and tortoise. The original Chinese qilin is a chimera — sources variously give it the body of a deer or ox, the tail of an ox, the hooves of a horse or cleft cloven hooves, scales of a fish or dragon, a dragon-like head, and one or two horns. Although Western translation often calls it the 'Chinese unicorn', the qilin is rarely depicted with a single horn; the name itself is a compound of 麒 (qí, the male) and 麟 (lín, the female). The earliest mention of the lin appears in the Classic of Poetry (Shijing, 11th–7th c. BCE), and the Spring and Autumn Annals records that Confucius identified a captured lin in 481 BCE shortly before his death — a passage central to later Confucian commentary. The kirin is gentle, will not walk on growing grass, will not eat living vegetation, and serves as an omen of the imminent birth or death of a great sage or ruler. In Japanese culture the kirin retains all this lore but is also the modern Japanese word for the giraffe — a usage that dates to the Ming dynasty, when the giraffes brought back from Africa by the eunuch admiral Zheng He's voyages (early 15th century) were celebrated at the Chinese court as living qilin. The figure is heavily represented in East Asian visual culture (komainu-style stone guardians, palace iconography, and the heraldry of Buddhist temples) and remains a major motif in modern manga, anime, and video games.

## Etymology

- **Origin**: Chinese 麒麟 (qílín), in which 麒 (qí) names the male and 麟 (lín) the female. Borrowed into Japanese as 'kirin', Korean as 'girin/kirin', and Vietnamese as 'kỳ lân'.
- **Meaning**: An auspicious chimerical beast; literally a male/female pair-word, treated as a single species name.
- **First use**: Earliest known reference is in the poem 'Lin zhi zhi' (麟之趾, 'Feet of the Lin') in the Classic of Poetry (Shijing), 11th–7th century BCE.

## Theories & Explanations

### Paranormal / Cryptozoological

**Genuine auspicious manifestation** _(Han-dynasty Confucian commentators, Imperial-era ritual specialists)_

In classical Confucian, Daoist, and East Asian Buddhist thought, the kirin is a genuine spiritual being whose appearance is an objective sign from heaven.

### Scientific

**Giraffe identification (Ming dynasty and after)** _(Sally K. Church, Modern East Asian historians)_

The Ming court's celebration of giraffes as living qilin reflects a real-world identification driven by the giraffe's exotic morphology — long neck, mottled coat, gentle disposition — and is the origin of the modern Japanese / Korean usage of kirin / girin for the giraffe.

**Sivatherium / extinct giraffid hypothesis** _(Various cryptozoological commentators)_

A minority popular hypothesis suggests that the qilin's chimerical features may preserve cultural memory of extinct giraffids such as Sivatherium; this is speculative and not mainstream paleontological consensus.

### Skeptical

**Mythical composite, not a real species** _(Standard East Asian mythography)_

Mainstream scholarship treats the qilin/kirin as a culturally constructed chimera assembled from familiar animal parts to symbolize virtue, harmony, and imperial legitimacy.
